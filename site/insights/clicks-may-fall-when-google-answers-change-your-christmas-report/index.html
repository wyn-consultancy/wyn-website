Clicks may fall when Google answers. Change your Christmas report

Your Christmas search report may show fewer clicks from the searches where people compare and buy, and part of that may be people getting their answer at the top of Google's page, which many reports don't count. Every figure here comes from US research; the source has no UK figures. UK teams should treat it as a prompt to check their own numbers before December's report goes out, and never as a finding about UK shoppers.

The research comes from Kevin Indig's quarterly AI Search Meta Analysis for Growth Memo, published on 16 November, which looks across 32 studies. Indig is careful to say that a single study tells you very little, and that the weight is in what a body of research points to.

What changed this year

Google's AI answer has moved into the searches that make money. As summarised in Indig's review, Semrush tracked 400 selected US desktop searches in banking, e-commerce and software, and found the AI summary on 45.75% of them in September 2025 and on 88% in August 2026.

That is a chosen sample of commercial searches, and the 88% counts each search once however often people make it, so it says nothing about all searches. The direction still matters: in his 2025 analysis, Indig found these answers appeared mainly on searches for information.

The second finding is about what happens when the answer shows, and it rests on one working paper that has not been peer reviewed. Indig's review reports that in a 2026 field experiment by Agarwal and Sen, clicks from the ordinary results out to websites fell by 39.8% when an AI answer appeared, and other coverage puts it at about 38%: close to 40% either way. Clicks on ads and how often people searched stayed the same. The test was randomised, which Indig says supports cause and effect.

A 2025 Semrush and Datos finding in the same review seems to point the other way: for searches that gained an AI answer, the share ending without any click fell from 33.75% to 31.53%. Indig notes that zero-click share differs from click-through rate.

The third is about where the sale is still won. In Attentive's August 2026 survey of 3,054 US adults who had recently browsed or shopped, 93% of those describing a recent shopping experience with an AI assistant said they looked for outside information or proof while deciding whether to buy. Shoppers' most-checked outside sources, by their own account, were customer reviews (37%), prices at other retailers (36%) and detailed product information (33%), Attentive reports. Much of that proof sits off your own site.

Why waiting is the risky choice

The shift is gradual. Indig says he found no evidence of people cutting their use of Google, so anyone telling you search is finished is ahead of the research.

What has moved is where the AI answer sits. It now appears on the searches where people compare and buy, and with Black Friday on 27 November, those searches are about to peak.

A team that judges search on clicks alone will read a fall this December as failure, and may cut the pages or the budget that still get the brand in front of shoppers. Leaving the report as it is and explaining the numbers in January is a decision too, and it is the one most likely to lead to the wrong cut.

There is a limit to how far this goes. Indig's verdict is that AI answers can reduce clicks, but the size of the effect depends on the format, the search and the context, and the studies don't support applying one percentage to every website. Be wary of anyone selling you a package built on a single figure, including the ones in this article.

One disclosure from the source: Semrush sponsors this issue of Growth Memo, and Indig notes that some of the research providers he cites sell AI search or analytics products.

What the report should count

For in-house e-commerce and marketing teams, clicks on their own stop being a fair test of whether search is working.

For agencies running search for clients, December's report is where this lands. The agency that has already split the numbers into separate lines walks into that meeting with an explanation. The one still reporting a single traffic figure walks in with a defence.

Indig's own advice is to track organic clicks, mentions and citations in AI answers, qualified visits, leads and sales as distinct outcomes. In plain terms, count how often people see you as well as how often they arrive, and keep both apart from sales.

What to do this week

Ask for this month's search report with separate lines. Clicks, mentions in AI answers, qualified visits and sales, each on its own. If clicks fall while sales hold, you will see it; with one blended number, you won't.

Ask someone to check what Google and an AI assistant say about you. Have them put your five most valuable product questions into both this week and screenshot what comes back. Indig recommends repeating important questions over time and checking how your brand is described; this gives you a January baseline.

Make the facts shoppers check easy to find, on your site and off it. Prices, delivery, returns and product details should be plain on the pages people land on, and your reviews and retailer listings should tell the same story. In Attentive's US survey, AI-assisted shoppers checked reviews, other retailers' prices and product details most, so your own pages are only part of the proof.

None of this needs a new tool or a new budget line. It needs someone senior to ask for a different report before the busiest weeks of the year, so the numbers in January mean something.
