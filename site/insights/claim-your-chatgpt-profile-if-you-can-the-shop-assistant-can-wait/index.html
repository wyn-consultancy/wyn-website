Claim your ChatGPT profile if you can. The shop assistant can wait

In a new beta, businesses can claim a verified profile in ChatGPT and correct the details, prices and stock on it. OpenAI announced it on 10 November, as The Next Web reported.

VentureBeat and the Free Press Journal, reporting OpenAI's announcement, say eligible businesses can claim a profile in markets where ChatGPT operates and OpenAI supports the required verification. The shop assistant inside the profile is US-only for now, open to eligible e-commerce and online-service firms. UK teams should still confirm in Business Hub.

Our advice comes in two parts. Claim the profile if you can, and name the person who keeps it right in the same week. Hold the money for the shop assistant until your own figures say it is worth it.

What OpenAI launched

The programme is called Businesses in ChatGPT (OpenAI's announcement). Through a tool called Business Hub, verified companies get a blue check and can update their details, prices and stock. They can also see how often ChatGPT mentions them, how many people click through to their website and the common topics in those conversations. OpenAI says it does not share users' chats with businesses.

The second part is the brand's own assistant, which OpenAI calls a Business Agent. When a business appears in a ChatGPT answer, people can open its profile and chat to that assistant, which answers from the company's product details. The chat is labelled and kept apart from ChatGPT's own answers.

That assistant is also an advertising product. Businesses can sponsor it as the landing spot for ChatGPT Ads, building on Sponsored Agents, which VentureBeat says OpenAI is testing with selected advertisers. OpenAI says paying does not change how often ChatGPT recommends a business (Digiday, via The Next Web), though that claim comes from OpenAI itself. For now, people who want to buy are sent to the brand's own website, while OpenAI builds payments with Visa.

Claim the profile, hold the assistant

ChatGPT already names brands when people ask what to buy and where to buy it. Ahrefs counted brand mentions across about 5.5 million US ChatGPT answers this month and found that a handful of brands are named again and again: Walmart appears in 1.73% of them, almost twice as often as second-placed Target. That is US data and tells you nothing about your own brand's share. It does show that being named in these answers is already a contest. Brands that claim a profile can at least correct the profile itself; the reports we read do not say whether that changes what ChatGPT says about them in other answers.

That is still the case for moving now. An unclaimed profile is one you can't correct, and the team that claims early learns how the profile and its figures behave before anyone has to decide on money. Waiting to see what competitors do is also a choice; it means they learn the system first.

The shop assistant is a bigger commitment. It speaks for the brand in conversations you don't see, and OpenAI's rules for it describe the work involved: it must say it is AI, keep prices accurate and avoid pressure tactics, and buying something or sharing personal data needs the user's explicit approval. While it hands people back to your website, what it adds is a conversation before the click. Treat any proposal to switch it on, or to sponsor it, like a new media buy: with a test, a target and a date to review it.

Who owns the facts

"Keep prices accurate" is the rule that matters most to marketing. Once a profile is claimed, the product details, prices and stock the business supplies are what the profile shows, under the brand's name and a blue check. A stale price there becomes a brand problem, and it lands with whoever owns the brand.

Where the product feed sits with e-commerce or IT, they should keep running it. The decisions about what the brand says, and the sign-off when a price or a description changes, belong in marketing, with an owner who has the time to check.

Agency clients will ask whether to claim, what to correct and whether to pay for the sponsored assistant. The useful answer separates the do-now part from the spend that needs evidence, and names the person on the client side who keeps the profile current.

The strongest case against rushing is upkeep. A claimed profile has to be maintained, and a profile with old prices and a blue check is worse than none. The figures in Business Hub also come from the platform reporting on itself. The Next Web notes the launch could squeeze start-ups such as Peec AI, which sell brands estimates of how visible they are in ChatGPT; read OpenAI's numbers as one view, not the only one.

So our condition is simple: claim the profile only if you name its owner in the same week. OpenAI says 1.2 billion people use ChatGPT each week. That is too many people to leave your prices to chance, and too new a channel to spend on before you have seen your own figures.

What to do this week

  • Ask someone to look your brand up in ChatGPT with the three questions customers most often ask you, and save screenshots of how it describes you and your prices.
  • Name one owner, inside marketing, for the product facts, prices and stock on your profile, with e-commerce or IT supporting.
  • Have that owner check in Business Hub whether your business can claim its profile. If it can, claim it and correct the details.
  • Before anyone buys a shop assistant or a sponsored placement, agree and write down the evidence that would justify it, for example a quarter of mention and click-through figures from the profile.
