AI shopping agents send little traffic. Get your checkout ready anyway

Retail clients will ask about AI shopping agents this Christmas: the assistants that can find a product, fill in the checkout and pay while the shopper approves from a chat window.

The honest answer has two parts. AI assistants send very little traffic, and nobody can show how many purchases agents complete. When an agent gets an order wrong, the refund and the dispute still belong to the seller. Neither is a good reason to do nothing.

How agents actually buy

The most useful account we have read is Okane Land's review of how AI agents buy from sellers, published on 14 October 2026. It went through the merchant pages and terms of the main shopping assistants and the card networks' rulebooks.

Its central finding: the agents that can buy today mostly do it the way a person does, on the seller's own checkout page and through the seller's own payment provider.

The clearest sign came in March. OpenAI stepped back from the checkout it had built into ChatGPT, saying the first version "did not offer the level of flexibility that we aspire to provide". ChatGPT now sends shoppers to finish on the merchant's own site. According to the review, Amazon's Buy for Me, Meta's Muse and OpenAI's newer dots agent also complete purchases on merchants' own sites.

The checkouts that do run inside a chat window, from Google, Microsoft and Perplexity, plus what remains of ChatGPT's, are for US buyers. The review doesn't say which agents work in the UK, so we won't guess. For UK clients the near-term question is simpler: could an agent finish a purchase on your site?

Disputes still land on the seller

Visa's rules (April 2026 edition) make the cardholder responsible for what their agent does "as if the Cardholder initiated the Transaction", and forbid a seller to make buyers give up their right to dispute a payment, agent purchases included. Stripe's agentic commerce seller terms (currently in preview) make a seller using that service the merchant of record, responsible for disputes, refunds and transaction fraud.

One small change helps sellers. From 24 October 2026, an agent provider's login ID can count as one of the matching details a seller shows to contest a Visa card-not-present fraud dispute. It adds a piece of evidence and leaves the risk where it was.

How small the numbers are

None of these is a UK figure we can confirm.

Okane Land's review cites a Frankfurt School study of 973 online shops between August 2024 and July 2025, which found ChatGPT and other LLM referrals made up under 0.2% of total traffic. That period ends before most of today's agent programmes launched. Ethercycle, a Shopify agency, tracked ten established Shopify stores from January to August 2026 (State of Ecommerce 2026): AI assistants brought 0.10% of sessions and 0.12% of revenue, about $131,000 out of $109.6 million. Direct handoffs from an agent to checkout were effectively zero.

Shoppers are wary too. In a YouGov poll fielded in December 2025, 14% of American adults said they trusted AI to place orders for them. In NMI's August 2026 survey of 1,000 US adults, 70% said it was important to be able to review or override a purchase before it goes through.

The counterweight: Adobe's AI traffic report, as reported by Digital Commerce 360, found that visitors sent to US retail sites by AI assistants converted 60% better than other traffic in July 2026. It doesn't give their share of traffic.

Why we'd act before the volume arrives

The tempting conclusion from those numbers is to park the subject until next year. We think that's a mistake, though the fix is much smaller than the hype suggests.

Because agents mostly buy through the seller's own checkout, the work that matters is ordinary e-commerce hygiene. A guest checkout and a saved wallet help a hurried human on a phone as much as an agent. That work pays off whether the agent volume turns up next Christmas or in three years. Waiting for the volume first means fixing a checkout an agent can't finish in a hurry, most likely in the middle of a trading peak.

For agencies, that makes a sensible AI shopping plan a short checklist on the client's own site. Skip the new channel strategy, and be sceptical of anyone selling a protocol project. The two main standards that let agents talk to shops, one from OpenAI and Stripe and one from Google and Shopify, are young, and the review notes that implementing OpenAI and Stripe's one doesn't list your products anywhere by itself.

What to check this week

  1. Make the checkout finishable by an agent. Let people buy without an account, leave sign-up until after payment, and accept a stored wallet such as Google Pay or Stripe's Link. Check with whoever runs the site that the host's bot protection lets through verified agents, the ones that identify themselves with a digital signature.
  2. Put plain facts on product pages. Show price, currency and stock status as text on the page, marked up with the standard product schema search engines already read. Google says its AI features need no new files or markup beyond that.
  3. Decide on AI crawlers on purpose. OpenAI separates the crawler that decides whether you appear in ChatGPT's search answers (OAI-SearchBot) from the one that collects training data (GPTBot). A client can let the first in and keep the second out.
  4. Keep dispute evidence. Order confirmations, login IDs, delivery addresses, IP addresses and device data. With Visa's change on 24 October, the agent provider's login ID joins that list.

Shopify says agentic storefronts are active by default for eligible stores, though its help page doesn't say which countries qualify. Check whether your store is eligible and switched on before anyone asks.
